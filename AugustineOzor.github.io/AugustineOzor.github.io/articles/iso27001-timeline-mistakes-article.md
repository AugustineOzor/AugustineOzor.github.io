# Six Timeline Mistakes That Kill ISO 27001 Programs

*By Augustine Ozor · 6 min read*

Most ISO 27001 programs don't fail because the team lacks skill. They fail on timing: work done in the wrong order, or all at once, or not at all. Auditors read a timeline the way an accountant reads a ledger. Here are six timeline mistakes that turn a good program into a list of findings, and what to do instead.

## Mistake 1 · Doing it all at once in the final weeks

**What it looks like.** Everything is backloaded into the weeks before the audit. Documents carry suspiciously similar timestamps. Processes look performed rather than operated.

**Why it hurts.** Auditors look for evidence that controls have run over time. A policy approved last Tuesday and a "monthly" review with one entry are easy to spot, and they erode trust in everything else you show.

**Fix.** Distribute the work evenly. Evidence accumulates from week 1, not from the last few weeks before the audit.

## Mistake 2 · Skipping internal audit

**What it looks like.** The team is behind schedule, so the internal audit is "folded into" the management review, or postponed until after certification.

**Why it hurts.** Clause 9.2 requires internal audits at planned intervals, and an ISMS without one cannot show it is being checked. Certification bodies typically treat a missing internal audit as a major nonconformity on its own.

**Fix.** Treat it as non-negotiable. Run the internal audit before the management review, always, because the review needs the audit results as an input.

## Mistake 3 · Implementing controls before the SoA

**What it looks like.** Controls are live, but no document explains why they are applicable, or why others were excluded.

**Why it hurts.** The Statement of Applicability is the link between risk and controls. Without it, every control is an unexplained choice, and the audit finding writes itself.

**Fix.** Keep the order: risk treatment, then the SoA, then control implementation. If a high-risk quick win cannot wait (multi-factor authentication is the usual example), implement it early but record the rationale and bring the SoA up to date at its approval gate.

## Mistake 4 · No buffer time

**What it looks like.** Every week is allocated. Then a workshop is cancelled, a vendor fails its assessment, and a key person resigns, all in the same month.

**Why it hurts.** A plan with no slack turns the first surprise into a missed date and the second into a compromised audit.

**Fix.** Reserve two to three weeks of unallocated time, place it before the readiness decision, and release it only with steering committee approval. This single habit protects the whole program.

## Mistake 5 · Treating evidence as a last-week task

**What it looks like.** The team spends the final week collecting screenshots, exporting logs and writing up records of things that happened months ago.

**Why it hurts.** Operating evidence shows a control working over a period of time. Evidence assembled at the end looks like it was, and auditors are trained to notice.

**Fix.** Design the evidence when you design the control: what record proves it works, who produces it, how often, and where it is stored. Plan for months of operating evidence rather than weeks, and make sure every quarterly or annual control has run at least once. How much history is expected varies by certification body, so confirm it with yours early.

## Mistake 6 · Letting scope drift mid-sequence

**What it looks like.** A new product, office or team is added after the scope has been signed off, "just this once".

**Why it hurts.** Scope sits at the top of the dependency chain. A change cascades through the risk assessment, the SoA, the controls and the evidence, and the rework is out of proportion to the original request.

**Fix.** Freeze the scope at sign-off. Route any later change through a formal change control process: impact assessment, leadership approval, and a documented update to every affected artifact. ISO 27001:2022 also asks you to plan changes to the ISMS in a controlled way (clause 6.3).

## Summary

| # | Mistake | Symptom | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Everything in the final weeks | Suspicious timestamps, theatre | Distribute work; evidence from week 1 |
| 2 | Skipping internal audit | Missing clause 9.2 evidence | Non-negotiable, before management review |
| 3 | Controls before SoA | Controls with no justification | Risk treatment, SoA, then implementation |
| 4 | No buffer | First surprise breaks the date | 2 to 3 weeks of approved slack |
| 5 | Last-week evidence | Records that don't cover the period | Design evidence with the control |
| 6 | Scope drift | Rework across risk, SoA and controls | Freeze scope; formal change control |

## The takeaway

Every one of these mistakes is a sequencing problem, which means every one is fixable on paper before it costs you in practice. Put the order, the gates and the buffer into the plan, and defend them when the pressure comes.
