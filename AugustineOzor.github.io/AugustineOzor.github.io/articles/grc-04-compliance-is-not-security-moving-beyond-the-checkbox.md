It is possible to be fully compliant and still be breached. Compliance frameworks set a minimum bar, are written for many organisations at once, and check that controls exist at a point in time. Attackers do not work from the same checklist.

## Signs of checkbox compliance

- Controls exist on paper but nobody checks whether they work.
- Evidence is gathered only in the weeks before an audit.
- Risks outside the framework's scope are ignored.
- The goal is "no findings" rather than "less risk".

## A better approach

Start from **risk**, then use frameworks to make sure nothing important is missed. Ask what could actually harm the business and its customers, design controls for those risks, and map them to the frameworks you must meet. One control can then serve several requirements at once.

Test controls for **operating effectiveness**, not just design. A documented access review process is good. Evidence that reviews actually happened every quarter, with exceptions fixed, is what matters.

Monitor **continuously** rather than annually, so a failing control is found in days, not at the next audit.

## Example

A clinic's policy requires encrypted laptops, which satisfies the auditor. A risk-based review finds that staff also export patient lists to personal cloud storage, which no checklist asked about. Fixing that gap is security; documenting it is compliance. Good GRC does both.

**Key takeaway:** Use compliance as the floor, not the ceiling.
