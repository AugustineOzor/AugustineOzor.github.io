**Having AI ethical principles is a good start. But principles alone are not enough.**

Many organisations have statements about fairness, transparency, accountability and responsible AI.

The real challenge is turning those principles into something people can consistently apply.

A practical AI ethical framework should answer questions such as:

- **Fairness:** What does fairness mean for this specific AI use case?
- **Transparency:** What information should be disclosed about the AI system's capabilities, limitations and use?
- **Harm:** Who could be affected, and what potential harms should be assessed?
- **Acceptable use:** What uses of the AI system are allowed, restricted or prohibited?
- **Accountability:** Who owns the risk, and who has authority to approve any residual risk?
- **Evidence:** What documentation, testing, monitoring or approval proves that the controls are actually working?

Without this level of clarity, different teams may interpret ethical principles differently. One team's definition of "fair" or "transparent" may be completely different from another's.

That creates inconsistency and makes AI governance difficult to audit and defend.

## Key takeaway

**AI ethics must be measurable, consistent and connected to controls.**

It is not enough to say "we develop and use AI responsibly."

Organisations need to be able to demonstrate what responsible AI means, who is accountable, what controls are required and what evidence proves those controls are working.

That is how AI ethics moves from principles to practice.
