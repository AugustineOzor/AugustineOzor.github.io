No set of controls prevents every incident. What separates a contained event from a crisis is preparation.

## Incident response

NIST SP 800-61 describes a lifecycle that most programmes follow:

1. **Preparation:** an incident response plan, defined roles, contact lists, tools and practised playbooks.
2. **Detection and analysis:** spotting incidents through monitoring and reports, and judging their scope and severity.
3. **Containment, eradication and recovery:** stopping the spread, removing the cause and restoring services safely.
4. **Post-incident activity:** a lessons-learned review that improves controls and the plan itself.

Regulated data adds deadlines. A HIPAA breach involving patient data, for example, triggers notification duties, so legal and privacy contacts belong in the plan from the start.

## Business continuity and disaster recovery

- A **business continuity plan (BCP)** keeps critical functions running during a disruption, such as clinicians switching to paper records during an outage.
- A **disaster recovery plan (DRP)** restores the IT systems themselves.
- A **business impact analysis** sets the targets: the **recovery time objective (RTO)**, meaning how quickly a system must be back, and the **recovery point objective (RPO)**, meaning how much data loss is tolerable.

## Test it

Plans only work if they are practised. Tabletop exercises, backup restore tests and failover drills reveal gaps while the stakes are low.

**Key takeaway:** Plan, practise and improve. The time to discover a gap in your response is during an exercise, not an incident.
