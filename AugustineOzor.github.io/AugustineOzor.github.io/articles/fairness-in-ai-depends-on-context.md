**Fairness in AI is easy to say, but harder to govern.**

One of the reasons is simple: fairness depends on context.

What fairness means for an AI system used in hiring may be very different from one used in healthcare, credit, insurance, education, fraud detection, or customer support.

This means organisations need to move beyond simply stating that “AI systems should be fair.”

A practical fairness policy should define:

- Who could be affected by the AI system
- What fairness metrics should be measured
- What thresholds are acceptable
- How often testing should take place
- Who reviews fairness results
- When issues should be escalated
- Who can accept residual risk

Accountability is equally important.

Business, legal, compliance, data, and technical teams need clear responsibilities for how fairness is assessed and managed.

These requirements should then flow into the AI lifecycle, from dataset review and testing to deployment approval, ongoing monitoring, and incident handling.

## Key takeaway

**Vague fairness policies can lead to inconsistent decisions and increased risk.**

Fairness becomes meaningful when organisations can define it, measure it, assign accountability, monitor it, and provide evidence that the required controls are working.

That is how fairness moves from an ethical principle to practical AI governance.
