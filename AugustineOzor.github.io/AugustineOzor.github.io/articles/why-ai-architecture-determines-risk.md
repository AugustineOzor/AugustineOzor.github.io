One of the biggest misconceptions about AI is treating it as a single technology.

In reality, **the way AI is adopted within an organization plays a major role in determining its risk profile, governance requirements, and security controls.**

Today, most enterprise AI falls into three broad categories:

🌐 **LLM Applications (Public AI)**
 Tools such as ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and public Copilot experiences that employees access directly.

Common challenges include:

🔹 Shadow AI
 🔹 Sensitive data being shared externally
 🔹 Limited visibility into prompts and usage
 🔹 Lack of enterprise-level monitoring and controls

☁️ **Embedded AI**

AI capabilities built into the platforms organizations already rely on, such as Microsoft 365 Copilot, Salesforce Einstein, or Snowflake Cortex.

The risk isn't necessarily the model itself. It's often:

🔹 Feature activation without adequate review
 🔹 Data exposure through existing permissions
 🔹 Limited visibility into internal SaaS data flows
 🔹 Shared responsibility between customer and vendor

⚙️ **Agentic AI (Homegrown AI)**

Custom-built AI systems, including:

🔹 AI assistants
 🔹 RAG applications
 🔹 Internal copilots
 🔹 Autonomous AI agents

Organizations gain more control over architecture, data, and workflows, but they also inherit more responsibility.

Key risks include:

🔹 Prompt injection attacks
 🔹 Data pipeline vulnerabilities
 🔹 Over-permissioned tools and agents
 🔹 Complex multi-step workflows
 🔹 Monitoring and observability challenges

### **Why This Matters**

The security and governance controls that work for one AI deployment model may not work for another.

A public AI application requires a different approach than an embedded SaaS feature or a custom-built AI agent.

That's why effective AI governance starts with understanding:

✅ Where the AI is running
 ✅ Who owns it
 ✅ What data it can access
 ✅ What actions it can perform
 ✅ Which controls are needed

### **Key Takeaway**

**The architecture you choose defines your risk surface.**

Public AI, Embedded AI, and Agentic AI each introduce different levels of visibility, control, and responsibility.

Successful AI governance is not about applying the same controls everywhere. It's about understanding the architecture and implementing the controls that fit the risk.
